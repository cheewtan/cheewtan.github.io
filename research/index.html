My research is on machine learning systems: theory, algorithms, optimization and software systems to make AI reliable, efficient and verifiable when deployed at scale. My group builds the layer between a model and the world it acts in, currently focusing on healthcare AI and AI-native wireless networks.

(1) Trustworthy LLM Agents and AI Systems

The central question in deploying LLM agents is not whether a model can answer, but whether the system around it can tell when it should not. Our recent work develops provenance-aware and evidence-state assessment for agentic retrieval-augmented generation [1], with its clinical instantiation described under Healthcare AI in (3). Earlier work built graph-based RAG for automated fact-checking (TrumorGPT) [2], surveyed the evaluation of LLMs [3], and probed the adversarial robustness of AI-generated-text detectors [4]. The common thread is treating the agent as a system with measurable failure modes and giving it the instrumentation to certify its own outputs. Current work turns the same lens on how the agents themselves are trained: a majorization-theoretic account of low-rank adaptation (LoRA) that explains which spectral directions a fine-tuning update should keep and which it can safely discard. The agents and copilots we build as software are described in (2).

(2) AI Agents and Copilots

Much of our work ships as software, and the software is where the systems questions become concrete. We build AI copilots for programming that retrieve context before they generate and test what they generate, from code generation through to software testing with context-based retrieval-augmented generation [1], a local-cloud copilot that routes between on-device and cloud models (CAMP) [2], and Copilot for Xcode, an early in-IDE assistant driven by cloud-hosted LLMs [3]. Underneath the copilots is the question of how to align a code-generation model with the people who use it, which we treat as Bayesian inference over crowd-sourced human feedback [4], and a review of natural language generation and understanding of big code for AI-assisted programming [5]. Current work extends the copilot into an autoresearch agent: an agentic loop in which an LLM proposes, runs, verifies and profiles its own experiments, with the automated generation of GPU kernels as the first testbed. These copilots are also the instruments of our education research in (9).

(3) Healthcare AI

Healthcare is where an AI system's efficiency must be optimized and AI safety is paramount. Our work spans two scales. At the scale of Electronic Health Record (EHR) data query, we build medical LLM systems that gate their own answers on evidence sufficiency [1] and ground them in provenance [2]. At the scale of populations, we work with clinical researchers on the spread of infectious diseases and antimicrobial resistance, developing AI-native decision intelligence for early warning, contact tracing and AI-health governance [3-5], building on a decade of work on contagion source detection and epidemic centrality that culminated in a Foundations and Trends monograph [6-8] where the same contagion mathematics governs infectious diseases and misinformation, and are useful to study future outbreaks.

(4) Optimization and Differentiable Programming

An idea that runs through all of our systems work is that an optimization algorithm can be turned into a learning architecture, and that the deep learning software stack is the place to build it. Differentiable programming, the paradigm behind PyTorch, JAX and Julia, treats a whole program as a computational graph and differentiates through it, so that an iterative solver, a fixed-point map or a physical simulation becomes a trainable module of the same software. We use it not only to execute optimization algorithms but to learn how to design them, embedding first-order methods and their Fenchel-Rockafellar duals inside these frameworks and training end to end (see the figure, from [1]). We develop learning to optimize by differentiable programming [1], in which a provably convergent algorithm is unrolled into a neural network whose parameters are trained end to end, so that the learned system inherits the algorithm's fixed-point structure; deep unfolding of fixed-point algorithms for weighted sum-rate maximization [2] is an instance, and the same construction underlies the DRL-driven block coordinate descent of (5). Our INFOCOM 2026 paper carries the idea into the radio access network: DIFFRACT neuralizes utility maximization over standard interference functions by differentiable programming, using the implicit function theorem to differentiate through the fixed point rather than unrolling it [3].

[1] L. Tao, X. Tong and C. W. Tan, Learning to Optimize by Differentiable Programming, arXiv 2601.16510, 2026.
[2] J. C. Riedel, C.-W. Tan and G. Caire, Deep Unfolding of Fixed-Point Based Algorithm for Weighted Sum Rate Maximization, IEEE International Symposium on Information Theory, 2025.
[3] C. W. Tan et al., DIFFRACT: Neuralized Utility Maximization for Wireless Networks by Differentiable Programming, IEEE INFOCOM, 2026.

(5) AI-Native Wireless Networks

In an AI-native network the optimizer is itself a learned system that must run in real time and come with a guarantee. Our approach is learning to optimize: unroll a provably convergent algorithm into a neural architecture, or train a reinforcement-learning policy to drive its block-coordinate steps, so that the learned system inherits the fixed-point structure of the algorithm. Recent results include deep reinforcement learning-based block coordinate descent for downlink weighted sum-rate maximization [1] and adversarial water-filling as a minimax resource-allocation foundation model [2-3]. The general methodology, learning to optimize by differentiable programming and deep unfolding of fixed-point algorithms, is developed in (4), and the fixed points it unrolls come from the wireless network optimization theory in (10).

[1] S. Chen, C. W. Tan and H. V. Poor, Deep Reinforcement Learning-Based Block Coordinate Descent for Downlink Weighted Sum-Rate Maximization on AI-Native Wireless Networks, IEEE Transactions on Wireless Communications, Vol. 25, pp. 3658-3674, 2026.
[2] X. Tong, C. W. Tan and H. V. Poor, Adversarial Water-Filling: Theory, Algorithms and Foundation Model, arXiv 2605.26163, 2026.
[3] X. Tong, C.-W. Tan and H. V. Poor, Adversarial Water-Filling: Minimax Resource Allocation Optimization with Proximal Decomposition in Open RAN, IEEE Globecom, 2025.

(6) Federated Learning and Privacy-Preserving Technologies

Training where the data lives means training under adversaries, churn and privacy obligations, and a federated system is only as trustworthy as the guarantees it can produce. We build federated and edge-learning systems that come with certificates: efficient and certified recovery of a global model from poisoning attacks, so that a compromised training run can be repaired rather than restarted [1]; primal-dual optimization that certifies the right to be forgotten for both samples and labels in vertical federated learning, turning unlearning into a verifiable claim [2]; and a credible and fair federated learning framework based on blockchain, in which contribution and reward are auditable [3]. Across these, the pattern is the one that runs through the rest of our work: a duality gap or a cryptographic record stands in for trust, and the system can show its work.

[1] Y. Jiang, J. Shen, Z. Liu, C. W. Tan and K.-Y. Lam, Towards Efficient and Certified Recovery from Poisoning Attacks in Federated Learning, IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, Vol. 20, pp. 2632-2647, 2025.
[2] Y. Jiang, X. Tong, Z. Liu, X. Zhang, K.-Y. Lam and C.-W. Tan, Certifying the Right to Be Forgotten: Primal-Dual Optimization for Sample and Label Unlearning in Vertical Federated Learning, IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, Vol. 20, pp. 13143-13158, 2025.

(7) Cloud Computing and AI Data Centers

AI is trained and served in data centers, and the questions of who pays for the computation and how fast it moves through the network are systems questions in their own right. Our earlier work developed the economics of cloud computation: how a user should bid for spot-priced cloud resources in online secondary markets such as Amazon Web Services [1], and when a virtual service provider that resells capacity is economically viable [2], with the MapReduce/Hadoop implementations released on GitHub. Two lines of work address the computation itself. For graph analytics at scale we developed parallel triangle-counting algorithms with pruning and hierarchical clustering on MapReduce for the DARPA-MIT-Amazon Graph Challenge [3]. Our recent work turns to the data-center network, accelerating distributed graph learning by moving multicast and aggregation into the network fabric [4], the kind of in-network computing that AI training clusters now depend on.

(8) Network Science of Misinformation and Infodemics

Who started it? We treat rumor source detection as statistical inference on a graph, with network centrality as the estimator, and have developed the maximum-likelihood theory and scalable algorithms for Markovian spreading models [1-3], and citation-network measures of scholarly novelty and viral influence [4]. Combined with LLM-driven fact-checking [5] and the analysis of political leaning from retweet networks [6], this gives an end-to-end pipeline from detecting a spreading falsehood to locating its origin; it shares its mathematics with the epidemic work in (3).

(9) AI in Education

AI assistance can build skill or replace it, and the difference is a systems question: what the tool withholds, when it intervenes, and how it measures whether the learner is still learning. We formalized this as an optimal knowledge-tracing problem for AI-assisted personalized education [1], and built and deployed the tools to study it: the programming copilots of (2), browser-sized agents that run small language models with WebLLM entirely in the student's browser for tool-augmented programming education [2], strategic LLM gaming agents for interactive learning [3-4], an LLM-driven generator of personalized assessment [5], and classroom-flipping designs that turn peer instruction and just-in-time teaching into an optimization problem [6-7]. Our study of ChatGPT and self-regulated learning [8] was among the earliest controlled classroom evidence on the question.

(10) Cyber-Physical Systems: Smart Grid and Wireless Networks

Before machine learning entered the loop, the systems we studied were physical networks whose control problems are nonconvex, and much of what we now do with learned optimizers rests on what we proved about them. In wireless networks, we developed the nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory of utility maximization under interference [1-4], which turned max-min SINR, outage-fairness and sum-rate problems into fixed-point computations with fast, provably convergent algorithms and resolved several long-open cases (e.g., Kandukuri and Boyd, TWC 2002); the monograph [1] gives the overview, and Matlab code is available for max-min SINR, outage fairness and sum-rate maximization. We extended the same tools to cognitive radio spectrum access and pricing [5-6], multiuser detection and space-time codes [7], and Internet routing, where we characterized the cost of not splitting flows and showed that sparse multipath routing recovers most of the optimal utility [8-9]. In the smart grid, we studied optimal power flow in resistive networks, and valley-filling algorithms for electric-vehicle charging, using data from a Southern California utility [10-11].
