I work on machine learning systems: the algorithms, optimization and system design that make AI reliable, efficient and verifiable when it is deployed at scale. My group builds the layer between a model and the world it acts in, from the provenance checks that decide whether an LLM agent should answer, to the learning-to-optimize methods that run inside a radio access network, to the federated and edge-learning protocols that train models where the data lives. We apply this to three domains where the cost of an unreliable system is high: healthcare AI, AI in education, and AI-native wireless networks. Underneath is a long-running programme on large-scale optimization, information theory and network science, much of it developed on cyber-physical systems such as wireless networks and the smart grid, which supplies the guarantees.

(1) Trustworthy LLM Agents and AI Systems

The central question in deploying LLM agents is not whether a model can answer, but whether the system around it can tell when it should not. Our recent work develops provenance-aware and evidence-state assessment for agentic retrieval-augmented generation [1], with its clinical instantiation described under Healthcare AI in (2). Earlier work built graph-based RAG for automated fact-checking (TrumorGPT) [2], studied how to align code-generation models with crowd-sourced human feedback through Bayesian inference and RLHF-style methods [3-4], surveyed the evaluation of LLMs [5], and probed the adversarial robustness of AI-generated-text detectors and deep hashing retrieval [6-7]. The common thread is treating the agent as a system with measurable failure modes and giving it the instrumentation to certify its own outputs. The certificate-based view of reasoning behind this work is developed in (7).

(2) Healthcare AI

Healthcare is where an AI system's uncertainty must be surfaced, not hidden. Our work spans two scales. At the scale of the individual clinical query, we build medical LLM systems that gate their own answers on evidence sufficiency [1] and ground them in provenance [2]. At the scale of populations, we develop AI-native decision intelligence for epidemic early warning and public-health governance [3], graph neural networks that learn to optimize contact tracing in epidemic networks [4], and machine-learning-enhanced graph analytics for infodemic risk management [5], building on a decade of work on contagion source detection and epidemic centrality that culminated in a Foundations and Trends monograph [6-8]. The same contagion mathematics governs disease and misinformation, which is why the two are studied together here; our algorithms were exercised on COVID-19 data and are designed to transfer to future outbreaks.

(3) AI in Education

AI assistance can build skill or replace it, and the difference is a systems question: what the tool withholds, when it intervenes, and how it measures whether the learner is still learning. We formalized this as an optimal knowledge-tracing problem for AI-assisted personalized education [1], and built and deployed the tools to study it: AI copilots for programming with context-based retrieval and local-cloud execution [2-4], strategic LLM gaming agents for interactive learning [5-6], an LLM-driven generator of personalized assessment [7], and classroom-flipping designs that turn peer instruction and just-in-time teaching into an optimization problem [8-9]. Our study of ChatGPT and self-regulated learning [10] was among the earliest controlled classroom evidence on the question. The programme runs live in the NTU College of Computing and Data Science, and was recognized with the 2025 IEEE Software Best Paper Award and the 2025 GMA AI Special Recognition Award.

(4) AI-Native Wireless Networks

In an AI-native network the optimizer is itself a learned system that must run in real time and come with a guarantee. Our approach is learning to optimize: unroll a provably convergent algorithm into a neural architecture, or train a reinforcement-learning policy to drive its block-coordinate steps, so that the learned system inherits the fixed-point structure of the algorithm. Recent results include deep reinforcement learning-based block coordinate descent for downlink weighted sum-rate maximization [1], neuralized spectrum access in Open RAN (OpenRANet) [2], and adversarial water-filling as a minimax resource-allocation foundation model [3-4]. The same tools handle task offloading and trajectory design in UAV-assisted mobile edge computing [5-6]. The general methodology, learning to optimize by differentiable programming and deep unfolding of fixed-point algorithms, is developed in (7), and the fixed points it unrolls come from the wireless network optimization theory in (8).

(5) Federated, Edge and Distributed Learning Systems

Training where the data lives means training under adversaries, churn and bandwidth limits. We build federated and edge-learning systems with certificates: certified recovery from poisoning attacks [1], primal-dual certification of the right to be forgotten in vertical federated unlearning [2], robust and fair frameworks that detect malicious clients and free-riders [3-4], and continual learning with adaptive multi-teacher distillation [5]. On the systems side, we accelerate distributed graph learning with collaborative in-network multicast and aggregation [6], plan model fine-tuning jointly with task serving for AI-defined vehicles [7], and survey coded federated learning for communication-efficient edge computing [8]. This line inherits from our earlier work on computing at scale: the economics of cloud and edge computation, including how to bid for spot-priced cloud resources and the viability of virtual service providers [9-10] (MapReduce/Hadoop code on GitHub), and scalable graph algorithms from the DARPA-MIT-Amazon Graph Challenge [11].

(6) Network Science of Misinformation and Infodemics

Who started it? We treat rumor source detection as statistical inference on a graph, with network centrality as the estimator, and have developed the maximum-likelihood theory and scalable algorithms for Markovian spreading models [1-3], the information-flow formulation of infodemic source detection [4], and citation-network measures of scholarly novelty and viral influence [5]. Combined with LLM-driven fact-checking [6] and the analysis of political leaning from retweet networks [7], this gives an end-to-end pipeline from detecting a spreading falsehood to locating its origin; it shares its mathematics with the epidemic work in (2).

(7) Optimization and Differentiable Programming

Two ideas run through all of our systems work: that an optimization algorithm can be turned into a learning architecture, and that an optimization solver can be turned into a proof. On the first, we develop learning to optimize by differentiable programming [1], in which a provably convergent algorithm is unrolled into a neural network whose parameters are trained end to end, so that the learned system inherits the algorithm's fixed-point structure; deep unfolding of fixed-point algorithms for weighted sum-rate maximization [2] and neural sum-rate maximization with algorithm unrolling and ADMM [3] are the wireless instances, and the same construction underlies the DRL-driven block coordinate descent and OpenRANet of (4). On the second, we introduced automated reasoning by convex optimization to prove or disprove information inequalities at scale, with a certificate for every verdict, released as the open-source AITIP solver [4-5] (featured in the IEEE Information Theory Society Newsletter, June 2020), and applied the same certificate-based method to the fundamental limits of interference channels and secure diversity coding [6-7]. Our current work joins the two ideas: LLMs that prove inequalities by pairing test-time compute with convex and signomial programming, and a maximum-entropy duality theory of counting in which first-order methods such as PDHG and accelerated proximal gradient produce a counting certificate at every iterate. The mathematics underneath, nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory and geometric programming, was developed on the physical networks in (8).

(8) Cyber-Physical Systems: Smart Grid and Wireless Networks

Before machine learning entered the loop, the systems we studied were physical networks whose control problems are nonconvex, and much of what we now do with learned optimizers rests on what we proved about them. In wireless networks, we developed the nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory of utility maximization under interference [1-4], which turned max-min SINR, outage-fairness and sum-rate problems into fixed-point computations with fast, provably convergent algorithms and resolved several long-open cases (e.g., Kandukuri and Boyd, TWC 2002); the monograph [1] gives the overview, and Matlab code is available for max-min SINR, outage fairness and sum-rate maximization. We extended the same tools to cognitive radio spectrum access and pricing [5-6], multiuser detection and space-time codes [7], and Internet routing, where we characterized the cost of not splitting flows and showed that sparse multipath routing recovers most of the optimal utility [8-9]. In the smart grid, we studied optimal power flow in resistive networks, valley-filling algorithms for electric-vehicle charging, and the decomposition of power networks with energy storage, using data from a Southern California utility [10-12] (see also UGC Research Frontiers). These are the fixed points, dualities and decompositions that the differentiable-programming work in (7) unrolls into neural architectures.
