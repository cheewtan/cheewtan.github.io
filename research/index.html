(1) Trustworthy LLM Agents and AI Systems

The question in deploying an LLM agent is not whether the model can answer but whether the system around it can tell when it should not. We develop provenance-aware evidence assessment for agentic retrieval-augmented generation [1], with a clinical instantiation in (3), graph-based RAG for automated fact-checking (TrumorGPT) [2], evaluation methodology for LLMs [3], adversarial robustness of AI-generated-text detectors [4], and risk awareness in LLM agents by probing their safety boundaries [5]. In each case the agent is treated as a system with measurable failure modes and given the instrumentation to certify its outputs. Current work studies low-rank adaptation (LoRA) from an optimization standpoint, asking what a fine-tuning update should preserve and what it can discard. The agents and copilots we build as software are described in (2).

(2) AI Agents and Copilots

Much of our work ships as software, and we are interested in how software is designed once language models are part of it, what some call Software 3.0: programs that are specified in natural language, generated and tested by models, and kept correct by the tools around them. We build AI copilots for programming that retrieve context before they generate and test what they generate. Our open-source copilot on GitHub, which carries code generation through to software testing with context-based retrieval-augmented generation [1], received the 2025 IEEE Software Best Paper Award; our open-source codebase has been assimilated into GitHub Copilot since March 2025 and is used by many developers. Alongside it are a local-cloud copilot that routes between on-device and cloud models (CAMP) [2] and Copilot for Xcode, an early in-IDE assistant driven by cloud-hosted LLMs [3]. Beneath the copilots is the question of how to align a code-generation model with the people who use it, which we treat as Bayesian inference over crowd-sourced human feedback [4], and a review of natural language generation and understanding of big code [5]. Current work extends the copilot into an autoresearch agent that proposes, runs, verifies and profiles its own experiments, with GPU-kernel generation as the first testbed. These copilots are also the instruments of our education research in (9).

(3) Healthcare AI

Healthcare is where an AI system's efficiency must be optimized and AI safety is paramount. Our work spans two scales. At the scale of Electronic Health Record (EHR) data query, we build medical LLM systems that gate their own answers on evidence sufficiency [1] and ground them in provenance [2]. At the scale of populations, we work with clinical researchers on the spread of infectious diseases and antimicrobial resistance, developing AI-native decision intelligence for early warning, contact tracing and AI-health governance [3-5], building on a decade of work on contagion source detection and epidemic centrality that culminated in a Foundations and Trends monograph [6-8] where the same contagion mathematics governs infectious diseases and misinformation, and are useful to study future outbreaks.

(4) Optimization and Differentiable Programming

Differentiable programming is the idea at the heart of deep learning: the paradigm behind PyTorch, JAX and Julia treats a whole program as a computational graph and trains it by backpropagation, so an iterative solver or a fixed-point map becomes a trainable module of the same deep learning software. We use it to learn how to design optimization algorithms, embedding first-order methods and their duals inside these frameworks and training end to end (see the figure, from [1]); first-order methods are also what make optimization GPU-accelerated, since their iterations are the matrix-vector products and proximal steps that GPUs run best. Our INFOCOM 2026 paper DIFFRACT neuralizes utility maximization over standard interference functions, differentiating through the fixed point by the implicit function theorem rather than unrolling it [2]. The same machinery serves as a proof engine: automated reasoning by convex optimization, solved with GPU-accelerated ADMM at the scale of millions of constraints, proves or disproves information inequalities and is released as the open-source AITIP solver [3].

[1] L. Tao, X. Tong and C. W. Tan, Learning to Optimize by Differentiable Programming, arXiv 2601.16510, 2026.
[2] C. W. Tan et al., DIFFRACT: Neuralized Utility Maximization for Wireless Networks by Differentiable Programming, IEEE INFOCOM, 2026.
[3] S. W. Ho, L. Ling, C. W. Tan and R. W. Yeung, Proving and Disproving Information Inequalities: Theory and Scalable Algorithms, IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, Vol. 66, No. 9, pp. 5522-5536, 2020 (AITIP open-source code).

(5) AI-Native Wireless Networks

In an AI-native network the optimizer is itself a learned system that must run in real time and come with a guarantee. Our approach is learning to optimize: unroll a provably convergent algorithm into a neural architecture, or train a reinforcement-learning policy to drive its block-coordinate steps, so that the learned system inherits the fixed-point structure of the algorithm. Recent results include deep reinforcement learning-based block coordinate descent for downlink weighted sum-rate maximization [1], deep unfolding of fixed-point algorithms for weighted sum-rate maximization [2], and adversarial water-filling as a minimax resource-allocation foundation model, which allocates power against worst-case channel uncertainty and applies to low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite networks, where channels change on the timescale of a satellite pass [3-4].

[1] S. Chen, C. W. Tan and H. V. Poor, Deep Reinforcement Learning-Based Block Coordinate Descent for Downlink Weighted Sum-Rate Maximization on AI-Native Wireless Networks, IEEE Transactions on Wireless Communications, Vol. 25, pp. 3658-3674, 2026.
[2] J. C. Riedel, C.-W. Tan and G. Caire, Deep Unfolding of Fixed-Point Based Algorithm for Weighted Sum Rate Maximization, IEEE ISIT, 2025.
[3] X. Tong, C. W. Tan and H. V. Poor, Adversarial Water-Filling: Theory, Algorithms and Foundation Model, arXiv 2605.26163, 2026.
[4] X. Tong, C.-W. Tan and H. V. Poor, Adversarial Water-Filling: Minimax Resource Allocation Optimization with Proximal Decomposition in Open RAN, IEEE Globecom, 2025.

(6) Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning

Training where the data lives means training under adversaries, churn, bandwidth limits and privacy obligations, and a federated system is only as trustworthy as the guarantees it can produce. We build federated and edge-learning systems that come with certificates: efficient and certified recovery of a global model from poisoning attacks, so a compromised run can be repaired rather than restarted [1]; primal-dual optimization that certifies the right to be forgotten for samples and labels in vertical federated learning [2], and efficient federated unlearning with adaptive differential-privacy preservation [3]; and a credible and fair federated learning framework based on blockchain, in which contribution and reward are auditable [4]. On the communication side, we surveyed coded federated learning for communication-efficient edge computing, where coding theory trades redundancy for straggler tolerance [5].

(7) Cloud Computing and AI Data Centers

AI is trained and served in data centers, and the questions of who pays for the computation and how fast it moves through the network are systems questions in their own right. Our earlier work developed the economics of cloud computation: how a user should bid for spot-priced cloud resources in online secondary markets such as Amazon Web Services [1], and when a virtual service provider that resells capacity is economically viable [2-3], with the MapReduce/Hadoop implementations released on GitHub. Three lines of work address the computation itself. For graph analytics at scale we developed parallel triangle-counting algorithms with pruning and hierarchical clustering on MapReduce for the DARPA-MIT-Amazon Graph Challenge [4]. For parallel computing with unreliable workers we showed that fault tolerance and network coding are the same problem, and derived optimal scheduling of coded tasks so that stragglers and failures do not stall the job [5]. Our recent work turns to the data-center and wide-area network: accelerating distributed graph learning by moving multicast and aggregation into the network fabric [6], the kind of in-network computing that AI training clusters now depend on, and learning the routing policies of data-center and backbone networks from observed traffic [7].

(8) Network Science of Misinformation and Infodemics

Who started it? We treat rumor source detection as statistical inference on a graph, with network centrality as the estimator, and have developed the maximum-likelihood theory and scalable algorithms for Markovian spreading models [1-3], and citation-network measures of scholarly novelty and viral influence [4]. Combined with the analysis of political leaning from retweet networks [5], this gives an end-to-end pipeline from detecting a spreading falsehood to locating its origin; it shares its mathematics with the epidemic work in (3).

(9) AI in Education

AI assistance can build skill or replace it, and the difference is a systems question: what the tool withholds, when it intervenes, and how it measures whether the learner is still learning. We formalized this as an optimal knowledge-tracing problem for AI-assisted personalized education [1], and built and deployed the tools to study it: the programming copilots of (2), browser-sized agents that run small language models with WebLLM entirely in the student's browser for tool-augmented programming education [2], strategic LLM gaming agents for interactive learning [3-4], an LLM-driven generator of personalized assessment [5], and classroom-flipping designs that turn peer instruction and just-in-time teaching into an optimization problem [6-7]. Our study of ChatGPT and self-regulated learning [8] was among the earliest controlled classroom evidence on the question.

(10) Network Optimization

Before machine learning entered the loop, the systems we studied were networks whose resource-allocation problems are nonconvex and whose decisions are made by many nodes with local information, and much of what we now do with learned optimizers rests on what we proved about them. Two themes run through this work: nonconvexity, and decentralized optimization in which each node updates using only what it can measure. In wireless networks under interference, we showed that power control can be posed and solved as geometric programming [1], and then developed the nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory of utility maximization [2-9], which turned a representative class of nonconvex problems on fairness and efficiency, from max-min SINR, max-min rate and outage fairness under Rayleigh fading to sum-rate maximization, into fixed-point computations with fast, provably convergent and naturally decentralized algorithms, resolving several long-open cases along the way (e.g., Kandukuri and Boyd, TWC 2002); the monograph [2] gives the overview and code is available on GitHub. The same fixed-point tools gave optimal spectrum management and network duality in cognitive radio networks [8, 10] and, for the Internet, a characterization of the cost of not splitting flows in routing [11]. In the smart grid, we studied optimal power flow in resistive networks and valley-filling algorithms for electric-vehicle charging in the smart grid [12-13].

(11) Coding Theory and Fundamental Limits

What is the best any scheme could do, and what does it cost to reach it? We study fundamental limits in communication and computing, and the codes that attain them. For distributed storage in cloud systems, we characterized the rate region of a class of secure asymmetric multilevel diversity coding systems, where data must be recoverable from any sufficient subset of nodes while leaking nothing to a limited eavesdropper [1]. The same idea applies to computation: treating stragglers and failures as erasures gives optimal scheduling of coded tasks, with an open-source implementation [2]. The figure illustrates the residue-number form of this principle, where a message stored as residues modulo coprime moduli is recovered from any sufficient subset by the Chinese remainder theorem, the algebraic structure that CRT decoding shares with linear network coding. In high-mobility wireless, we showed how the algebraic structure of Alamouti space-time codes yields low-complexity multiuser detection [3], and for interference channels we established the maximum achievable sum rate under successive decoding, which bounds what any such receiver can deliver [4].

[1] C. Li, X. Guang, C. W. Tan and R. W. Yeung, Fundamental Limits on a Class of Secure Asymmetric Multilevel Diversity Coding Systems, IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications, Vol. 36, No. 4, pp. 737-747, 2018.
[2] C. Li, Y. Zhang and C. W. Tan, Fault-Tolerant Computation Meets Network Coding: Optimal Scheduling in Parallel Computing, IEEE Transactions on Communications, Vol. 71, No. 7, pp. 3847-3860, 2023 (open-source code).
[3] C. W. Tan and A. R. Calderbank, Multiuser Detection of Alamouti Signals, IEEE Transactions on Communications, Vol. 57, No. 7, pp. 2080-2089, 2009.
